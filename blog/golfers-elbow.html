Golfer’s Elbow: Why It Hurts and What Actually Fixes It

Dr. Jeremy Velasco, PT, DPT

Owner, Elite Golf PT  ·  October 3, 2026  ·  6 min read

The pain shows up on the inside of your elbow, usually in the trail arm, and it is worst when you grip a club or pick up a coffee cup. Most golfers wait it out, because every article they find says rest. Months later it is still there. Rest is usually the wrong treatment, and the reason why says a lot about what golfer’s elbow actually is.

What golfer’s elbow actually is

The muscles that curl your wrist and rotate your palm downward all share one anchor point: a bony bump on the inner side of your elbow called the medial epicondyle. Golfer’s elbow, known clinically as medial epicondylalgia, is pain where that shared tendon attaches.

The older name is medial epicondylitis, and that suffix is the source of a great deal of wasted time. “Itis” means inflammation, and for decades the condition was treated as an inflammatory one: rest it, ice it, take an anti-inflammatory, wait for the swelling to settle. When researchers looked closely at the tissue itself, they largely did not find inflammatory cells. What they found was disorganized, degraded collagen. The tendon had not become inflamed. It had become disorganized.

That changes the entire treatment logic. Inflammation calms down when you leave it alone. A disorganized tendon does not reorganize on its own, because the signal that tells it to rebuild is load. Take all the load away and the tendon gets weaker, not stronger. This is why so many golfers rest for six weeks, feel fine, swing a club, and are back to square one within two rounds.

Rest takes the pain away without fixing the tendon. The pain comes back because the tendon never changed.

The name is misleading in two directions

First, most people with golfer’s elbow have never played golf. It is common among plumbers, carpenters, chefs, climbers, and anyone who grips hard and repeatedly. The name stuck for historical reasons, not because golf is the leading cause.

Second, and more useful if you do play: golfers get tennis elbow more often than golfer’s elbow. Tennis elbow is the same kind of problem on the outer side of the elbow, where the wrist extensors attach. In a right-handed golfer it tends to show up in the lead arm, the left, while golfer’s elbow shows up in the trail arm, the right.

So before anything else, be sure which one you have. Press on the bony bump on the inside of your elbow, then the one on the outside. The tender one tells you which tendon is involved. The two are treated along similar lines, but knowing which side is overloaded matters when you start looking at your swing.

Why golf does it

A golf swing asks the forearm flexors to do two demanding things at once. They hold grip pressure through a motion that is trying to pull the club out of your hands, and they stabilize the wrist through impact. Impact is the moment that matters, because impact is where the force spikes.

Four things reliably raise that spike:

  • Hitting the ground before the ball. A fat shot sends a shock up the shaft that the forearm has to absorb. Players who take heavy divots, or who fight chunked shots, load the tendon far harder than their scorecard suggests.
  • Mats. A range mat does not let the club dig. Hit slightly behind the ball on turf and the club slides. Do it on a mat and it bounces, straight back into your arm. Winter range sessions are a classic trigger.
  • Grip pressure. Squeezing harder keeps the flexors firing through the whole swing instead of just impact. Grip size plays into this, since a grip that is too small encourages a tighter hold.
  • A sudden jump in volume. Tendons adapt to load slowly. The problem is rarely how much you play. It is how quickly that amount changed, which is why this often appears early in the season or after a golf trip.

The part that gets missed

Here is the pattern I see most often in the clinic. The elbow is where it hurts, but the elbow is rarely where the problem started.

If your shoulders and mid-back do not rotate well, the club still has to get around your body somehow, and the arms and hands end up supplying the motion the torso could not. If you release the club early, a move often called casting, the wrists do more work through impact than they should. In both cases the forearm is being asked to contribute power it was never built to produce, swing after swing.

That is also why two golfers can practice identically and only one ends up with symptoms. The tendon is not failing because the sport is unreasonable. It is failing because of how the load is being distributed long before it reaches the arm. This is the same story as lower back pain in golf, where the painful area is usually compensating for something further away.

The elbow is the place that complains. It is not usually the place that is wrong.

What actually works

Current evidence on tendon problems points consistently in one direction: progressive loading. The goal is to give the tendon a reason to rebuild, which means working it, carefully and gradually, rather than protecting it.

1

Settle it down without shutting it down

Stop the specific thing that spikes the pain, which is often range sessions on mats rather than golf itself, but keep using the arm. Complete rest is the thing to avoid. Isometric holds, where you load the wrist flexors and simply hold without moving, are often well tolerated early and can take the edge off for a few hours.

2

Load it progressively

Slow, controlled wrist flexion and forearm rotation work with a light dumbbell, built up over weeks. The key words are heavy enough to matter and slow enough to control. Mild discomfort during the work is expected and acceptable; sharp pain, or pain that is clearly worse the next morning, means the dose was too high.

3

Fix what sent the load there

Grip strength, shoulder and thoracic rotation, and the swing pattern itself. This is the part that decides whether it stays fixed. Loading the tendon without changing what overloaded it tends to buy you a comfortable off-season and a painful spring.

4

Check the equipment

Grip size, shaft stiffness, and how much of your practice happens on mats. These are small changes that remove load permanently, which makes them worth more than they sound.

How long it takes

Longer than you want. Tendons remodel on a timescale of months rather than weeks, and these cases commonly take somewhere in the range of three to six months to settle properly. Some resolve faster. Ones that have been grumbling for a year take longer still.

Knowing that up front matters, because the most common reason this rehab fails is not that the plan was wrong. It is that people stop at week four, when the pain has faded but the tendon has not finished changing.

When it is not just a tendon

A few symptoms mean this is worth having looked at properly rather than managing on your own:

  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in your ring and little fingers. The ulnar nerve passes right behind the medial epicondyle, and nerve symptoms are a different problem with a different treatment.
  • Pain that wakes you at night, or pain that is not clearly tied to using the arm.
  • A specific moment of injury, especially with swelling, bruising, or a sense that something gave way.
  • No change after three months of genuinely consistent loading work.

The short version

Golfer’s elbow is a tendon that has lost its structure, not a joint that is inflamed. Rest relieves it temporarily and changes nothing. Loading it progressively is what rebuilds it, and working out why the forearm was doing so much of the job in the first place is what keeps it from coming back.

If you have been resting it for months and it returns the moment you play again, that is not bad luck. It is a sign the plan was treating the wrong thing.
