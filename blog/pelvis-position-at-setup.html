Where Your Hips Sit at Address, and Why It Changes Your Strike

Dr. Jeremy Velasco, PT, DPT

Owner, Elite Golf PT  ·  September 6, 2026  ·  5 min read

Most golfers who come to me convinced they need to rebuild their swing are actually losing strokes somewhere much earlier: the half-second before they move at all. Where your hips sit over your feet at address quietly sets up everything that follows, and it is one of the easiest things in golf to change.

The string test

Picture a weighted string hanging straight down from your belt buckle while you stand at address. Builders use a version of this tool, called a plumb bob, to check whether a wall is truly vertical. Golf coaches borrowed the idea to answer one simple question: when that string drops, does it land behind the middle of your stance, right on it, or ahead of it?

That is the whole test. No equipment, no numbers, no lab. Just where your hips sit relative to the center of your feet. And the answer should not be the same for every club in your bag.

Your setup is not one position you memorize. It is a dial you turn depending on what the club is being asked to do.

Why the same stance cannot work for every club

Every swing has a lowest point, the spot where the clubhead comes closest to the ground. Where that low point falls, relative to the ball, is most of what people mean when they talk about striking it well. And your hips are one of the biggest levers you have for moving it.

The ball is teed up off the ground with a driver, and you want the clubhead traveling slightly upward when it arrives. That is easier when your hips sit a touch further back, behind the center of your stance, because the low point tends to move back with them.

An iron off the turf is the opposite problem. You want to catch the ball first and the ground second, which means the low point needs to be a little ahead of the ball. The hips shift toward the target to help make that happen.

Wedges ask for the most of all. A short, steep, descending strike wants the low point furthest forward, so the hips sit furthest forward too. Same body, same swing, three different starting positions.

Here is where it gets useful. If you strike your wedges beautifully but thin your driver, or the other way around, you may not have two swing problems. You may have one setup that you carry across the whole bag whether it suits the club or not.

The mistake almost everyone makes

When golfers hear "move your hips toward the target," most do it by leaning. The upper body tips away from the target, one shoulder drops, and the spine bends sideways to create the appearance of a hip shift without much real hip shift underneath it.

That is a different position than the one you were going for, and it comes with a cost. Side-bending the spine and then swinging hard through it is one of the more reliable ways I know to end up with a sore lower back. What you actually want is the pelvis staying level while it moves toward the target, more like sliding a drawer than tilting a seesaw.

Try it yourself

1

Film yourself from the front

Phone on the ground, camera facing you straight on, taking your normal address position. Do it with a driver, a mid iron, and a wedge. Do not adjust anything yet. You want your honest, default setup.

2

Draw the line

Pause each frame and trace a vertical line straight down from your belt buckle. Compare where it lands against the midpoint between your feet. Most people are surprised to find all three clubs look nearly identical.

3

Change one club at a time

Pick your worst-striking club and adjust only that setup, in small increments, keeping your belt line level as you go. Hit a handful of balls. If contact improves, you have found something worth keeping.

When your body will not let you

This is the part that usually gets skipped, and it is the part I care about most as a physical therapist.

A setup position is only available to you if your joints allow it. Moving your hips toward the target while keeping your pelvis level asks for a certain amount of hip mobility, some ankle range, and enough control through your midsection to hold the shape while you swing. If any of that is missing, your body does not simply refuse. It finds a workaround, and the workaround is usually the sideways lean described above.

So a golfer can be given entirely reasonable advice, try honestly to apply it, and end up in a worse position than they started, with a sore back to go with it. The instruction was not wrong. The body just could not deliver it yet.

You cannot hold a position your body has not been given the range to reach.

That is what a movement screen is for. Before changing anyone's setup, I want to know what their hips, ankles, and spine can actually do, so we are asking for a position their body can hold rather than one it has to fake. Sometimes the answer is a few weeks of mobility work first. Sometimes the range is already there and it is purely a matter of awareness, which is a very good day for everyone.

If you want the longer version of why hips drive so much of this, I wrote about that separately in Hip Mobility for Golf.

The bigger point

Setup changes are the least invasive thing you can do to a golf swing. Nothing about your motion has to change, there is no long rebuild, and the feedback comes immediately. Before you tear apart a swing that mostly works, it is worth checking whether the problem starts before you ever move.

And if you try the adjustment and your body fights you on it, that is not a failure. That is information, and it may be the most useful thing this test can tell you.
