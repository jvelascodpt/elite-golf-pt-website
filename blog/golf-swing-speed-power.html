Power & Speed

Golf Swing Speed: Where Your Power Really Comes From

Dr. Jeremy Velasco, PT, DPT, CPT, TPI

Owner, Elite Golf PT  ·  November 23, 2026  ·  7 min read

Almost every golfer wants more distance, and most go looking for it in the bag: a new driver, a different shaft, a hotter ball. Equipment can help at the margins, but the engine behind clubhead speed is your body. How much force you can make, how fast you can make it, and how well you pass it from the ground to the club decide how fast that club is moving at impact. When golfers lose speed, the cause is usually physical, and so is the fix.

Speed is built from the ground up

A powerful swing is a chain reaction. Your feet push against the ground, the hips start to turn, the torso follows, then the arms, and finally the club. Each segment speeds up and then slows down, handing its energy to the next one, a bit like cracking a whip. Researchers and coaches call this the kinematic sequence.

When the sequence works, each link adds speed to the one after it and the club arrives at the ball faster than any single part of your body could move it. When a link is weak, stiff, or out of order, energy leaks out of the chain. The swing can feel fast and effortful while the club is moving slower than it should.

Swinging harder rarely adds speed. Removing the leaks in the chain usually does.

5 places golfers leak speed

In our clinic, lost speed almost always traces back to one or more of these:

  • Limited rotation. If your hips or mid-back cannot turn, your backswing gets short and there is less room to build speed on the way down. We cover this in more detail in hip mobility for golf and thoracic mobility for golf.
  • Weak use of the ground. The legs are the biggest and strongest muscles you have. If they are not strong or explosive enough to push into the ground, the swing has to be powered by the arms.
  • Poor sequencing. The upper body or arms start the downswing before the hips. Everything fires at once instead of in order, and the whip never cracks.
  • An unstable base. Swaying, sliding, or losing your posture lets energy drain sideways instead of being turned into rotation.
  • Lost power with age. As we get older, the ability to produce force quickly tends to decline faster than plain strength does. Many golfers stay strong enough to lift things but lose the explosiveness that creates speed, unless they train it on purpose.

Why chasing speed can get you hurt

Swing speed programs and overspeed training are everywhere, and they can work. The problem is adding speed to a body that is not ready for it. If the hips or mid-back cannot rotate, the extra force goes to the joints that are left: the lower back, the lead shoulder, the elbows. Golfers who chase speed without fixing the limitation first often trade a few yards for a new injury.

That is why we do not start with speed. We start by finding out what your body can and cannot do, then build speed on a foundation that can handle it.

What building speed looks like

For most golfers the order matters: move well, then get strong, then get fast. Mobility and control come first so the body can reach the positions a powerful swing needs. Strength comes next, especially in the legs and trunk, so you have force to work with. Power and speed training come last, teaching you to produce that force quickly and in the right order.

Golfers who follow that order commonly find that speed comes with less effort, their swing feels more connected, and they can keep it up for a full round without the aches that used to follow a hard practice session.

If pain is already part of your game, that comes first. Our golf and lower back pain article explains why the sore spot is often paying for a limitation somewhere else.

The short version

Clubhead speed comes from your body working as a chain, from the ground up. Lost speed usually means a link in that chain is stiff, weak, or firing out of order. Measuring each link shows you where your speed is going, and fixing the right one first is the safest way to get it back.
